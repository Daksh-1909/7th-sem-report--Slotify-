ABSTRACT
Walk into any small salon, clinic, or tutoring center in India and you will still find the
same scheduling setup that has existed for decades – a register on the front desk, a
phone that rings constantly, and someone trying to remember which slot was given to
which person on which day. The problems that come out of this are predictable: double
bookings, forgotten appointments, no way to know how many clients are expected next
Tuesday, and no automated way to remind anyone about their visit. We talked to a salon
owner in Vadodara’s Alka Puri area early in the project and she put it plainly – she
was losing about three bookings a week not because clients were irresponsible, but
because there was no easy way for them to cancel and let someone else take the
slot. That conversation set the direction for everything that followed.
Slotify is a web-based appointment scheduling platform built around that specific
problem. Customers get a clean interface to search for service providers, check realtime slot availability, and book or reschedule appointments without making a phone call.
Service providers get a dashboard for managing staff, working hours, and upcoming
appointments, plus basic analytics about how their business is actually running.
The platform is built on the MERN stack. We initially considered PostgreSQL because
the team was more comfortable with relational databases, but switched to MongoDB
mid-design when we realised that a two-doctor clinic and a three-chair salon store
structurally different data – forcing both into one rigid schema would have meant constant
migrations as requirements evolved. Authentication uses JWT tokens and Google OAuth.
Appointment confirmations go out immediately through Nodemailer, and a 24-hour
reminder follows automatically.
The whole point of Slotify is that the right combination of features for small service
businesses has not been available at a price point that makes sense for them. Future
extensions include mobile apps, payment integration, and an AI-based slot recommendation engine.

CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background and Motivation
Most people who have tried to book an appointment at a local clinic or salon know the
routine. You call, wait on hold, confirm a time, write it somewhere, and hope the other
side remembers too. Half the time it works. The other half, you arrive to find the slot was
double-booked, or you forgot entirely because nobody sent a reminder. Both outcomes
are avoidable. Neither has been fixed, at least not for the kinds of businesses this project
is aimed at.
When we asked a salon owner in Vadodara’s Alkapuri area why she still kept a physical
register despite knowing that digital tools existed, her answer was not what we expected.
It wasn’t that she didn’t know better options were out there. It was that every tool she’d
tried still required someone to manually call no-shows the next day and free up those
slots – which defeated most of the point. The automation stopped exactly where she
needed it most. The dead-slot problem – a customer cancels two hours before their
appointment, there’s no waiting list, no push notification, the slot sits empty and the staff
member waits – was costing her three bookings a week that she had no clean way to
recover. That conversation, more than any paper we read, shaped what Slotify became.
The data visibility problem compounds this in a way that’s easy to miss until you look
for it. The same paper register that can’t send reminders also can’t tell her which
days are consistently quiet, which services get cancelled most, or whether a particular
staff member is generating repeat bookings. The information exists somewhere in the
notebooks, but extracting it would take an hour she doesn’t have. For small service
businesses, the absence of operational data isn’t a separate problem from the absence
of digital scheduling – it’s the same problem, because both trace back to the same root
cause: no system.

1.2 Problem Statement
Manual coordination overhead and appointment attrition are two sides of the same
issue, and it helps to treat them together rather than as a neat three-category list. When
bookings are managed over phone calls, every transaction requires a human in the loop
– confirming, recording, checking for conflicts. That works at low volume. The same
process that handles ten appointments a day breaks visibly at thirty, and there’s no
graceful failure mode: things just get dropped.
The no-show and dead-slot problem compounds this directly. Studies on scheduling
behaviour in outpatient healthcare have documented no-show rates of around 23% in
settings without any reminder system – a figure from a prospective randomised trial
of over 9,000 outpatients [6]. Service businesses outside healthcare face the same
dynamic – the salon owner’s three lost bookings per week is consistent with that data –
but almost never have the infrastructure to address it. More importantly, the infrastructure
needed to send a reminder is the same infrastructure needed to recover a cancelled
slot: a system that knows about appointments in real time. Fixing the reminder problem
and fixing the dead-slot problem are the same fix.

1.3 Project Objectives
1. Build a customer-facing booking interface where users can search providers,
see real-time slot availability, and complete a booking without any phone-based
coordination – including a three-nearest-alternative prompt when a chosen slot is
taken.
2. Develop a provider dashboard that handles schedule configuration, staff assignment, split-shift support (something we found extremely common in small clinics
but largely absent from tools like Calendly), and automatic double-booking prevention at the database level rather than just the UI level.
3. Implement an automated notification pipeline: confirmation email at the point of
booking, reminder email at T-24 hours. The evidence that reminders produce
meaningful improvement comes from [6]; the 24-hour timing specifically is a UX
design choice rather than an evidence-based optimisation – Chapter 2 explains
why.
4. Integrate calendar synchronisation and deploy to cloud infrastructure together,

5. Provide business analytics covering appointment trends, cancellation rates, and

1.4 Scope
The current scope covers service businesses that operate on a time-slot model – clinics,

LITERATURE SURVEY
2.1 Overview
One of the five papers reviewed here we almost didn’t read. One turned out to describe
its methodology very differently from how it is usually cited elsewhere. One we disagreed
with on a specific implementation recommendation. Those differences show up in how
much space each review gets – the point is not balanced coverage but an honest account
of what we actually found and how it changed what we built.

2.2 Paper Reviews
2.2.1 Cayirli and Veral (2003) – Outpatient Scheduling Models
Source: Cayirli, T. and Veral, E., 2003. Outpatient scheduling in health care: a review of
literature. Production and Operations Management, 12(4), pp. 519–549 [2].
We almost didn’t read this. The title says healthcare, the data is entirely from hospital
outpatient departments, and our system is targeting salons and tutoring services. What
changed our mind was reading the methodology section carefully. The paper is a fortyyear review of scheduling algorithms and models – it covers individual-slot systems,
block scheduling, open-access scheduling, and hybrid models, measuring each against
wait time, utilisation rate, and throughput. It is an operations management paper that
happens to be set in hospitals, not a clinical paper.
The review’s core argument – that point-of-booking conflict management improves both
utilisation rates and patient satisfaction compared to post-booking resolution – is one we
found applicable to our context. The specific claim is about scheduling structure rather
than healthcare specifically: when you enforce conflict checks at the moment of booking,
the downstream problems (double-bookings, scheduling gaps, last-minute cancellations
caused by errors) reduce measurably.
What we couldn’t verify is whether the specific utilisation improvements they document
hold for salons or tutoring services. Cayirli and Veral [2] are explicit that their dataset is
entirely clinical. We searched Google Scholar for “appointment no-show rate salon India”
5

and “service booking behaviour small business India” and found nothing peer-reviewed
– a few industry blog posts, one McKinsey market sizing report, no controlled studies.
We made the assumption that the structural dynamics are similar enough, but it is an
assumption rather than a finding.
What it changed in Slotify: The slot generation logic uses fixed-duration windows
with configurable break buffers – the structure the paper identifies as most practical for
variable-service-duration settings.
2.2.2 Parikh et al. (2010) – A Randomised Trial of Appointment Reminders
Source: Parikh, A. et al., 2010. The effectiveness of outpatient appointment reminder
systems in reducing no-show rates. The American Journal of Medicine, 123(6), pp.
542–548 [6].
This paper is frequently cited in summaries as a “review of 21 studies,” but that is a
mischaracterisation worth correcting because it changes how you read the results. The
actual paper is a single prospective randomised controlled trial conducted at the Robert
Wood Johnson University Medical Group. 12,092 patients were randomised across three
groups: NONE (no reminder, n=3,350), AUTO (automated phone call, n=3,219), and
STAFF (call placed by front desk staff, n=3,266). Both reminder groups were contacted
three days (72 hours) before the appointment.
The results: the NONE group had a no-show rate of 23.1%. The AUTO group: 17.3%.
The STAFF group: 13.6%. In absolute terms, the automated reminder reduced no-shows
by 5.8 percentage points compared to no reminder at all. The staff-placed call reduced
them by 9.5 percentage points. Both improvements were statistically significant.
This is worth stating precisely because the statistic is often reported as a relative
reduction – 5.8 percentage points off a 23.1% baseline is roughly a 25% relative
reduction, which sounds more dramatic than the absolute figure but describes the same
data. Both are true; they just communicate different things.
An important limitation for our use case: the paper only tested one reminder timing
window (72 hours in advance). It does not contain data about whether 24-hour versus
48-hour reminders perform differently – that comparison was not part of the trial design.
Our decision to send reminders at T-24 hours rather than T-72 was therefore not directly

grounded in this paper. It was a design choice based on general UX reasoning: a
reminder that arrives the morning before an appointment feels more actionable than one
that arrives three days out. Both Cayirli & Veral [2] and Parikh et al. [6] confirm that any
reminder system produces meaningful improvement; the exact timing is something we

choice, not a finding from this paper.
2.2.3 Richardson and Ruby (2007) – RESTful Web Services
Source: Richardson, L. and Ruby, S., 2007. RESTful Web Services. O’Reilly Media [8].
The book is nearly two decades old, and some of its REST recommendations predate
the widespread adoption of JSON – much of the practical content uses XML, which
required interpretation in a modern context. The core architectural argument holds,
though: a stateless, resource-oriented API (originally formalised in Fielding’s [3] doctoral
dissertation) that separates client from server produces systems that are easier to
extend and test independently.
The specific principle we applied is resource addressability – every entity has a unique
URI, and actions are expressed through HTTP methods rather than custom endpoint
names. A booking is not cancelled through /cancelAppointment but through DELETE
/api/appointments/:id. We had a version of the API that used verb-based endpoints

(/confirmBooking, /rejectBooking) and the refactor to resource-based routing made
the code considerably cleaner. Richardson and Ruby [8] predicted exactly that outcome,
which in retrospect was obvious, but we had to make the mistake first.
2.2.4 Sadalage and Fowler (2012) – NoSQL Distilled
Source: Sadalage, P.J. and Fowler, M., 2012. NoSQL Distilled. Addison-Wesley [9].
We started the data modelling phase working in PostgreSQL, which was a mistake we
had to discover. The problem Sadalage and Fowler [9] predict – that schema-heavy
relational databases struggle when instances of the same type store structurally different
data – is exactly what we ran into. A clinic appointment needs a diagnosis field and a
consultation type. A salon appointment needs a stylist, a product, and a colour code.
7

Slotify – Smart Appointment Booking System

Forcing both into a shared appointments table produced either a wide nullable schema
or a separate metadata join table that grew unwieldy. Reading NoSQL Distilled after that
experience felt like reading a post-mortem of our own mistake. MongoDB’s document
model let each business type carry exactly the fields it needs without imposing shared
structure, which is what prompted the switch.
What the book is less helpful on is query performance tuning, where we had to look
elsewhere – primarily MongoDB’s own documentation [13]. The compound index on
(businessId, dateTime, status) wasn’t in the book; we worked that out empirically

when slot queries started slowing down with test data of around 5,000 appointments.
2.2.5 Madden (2020) – API Security in Action
Source: Madden, N., 2020. API Security in Action. Manning Publications [5].
The JWT chapter is the one that mattered. Madden is clear that JWTs should be
short-lived, signed with a strong secret, and should never carry sensitive data in the
payload – because the payload is base64-encoded, not encrypted, and readable by
anyone who intercepts the token. An early version of our auth system stored the user’s
full name and email in the JWT payload, which the book flags explicitly as a bad pattern.
We stripped it back to just user ID and role.
The chapter on localStorage versus httpOnly cookies was the one we ended up partially
disagreeing with – for reasons we are honest about in Chapter 4. Madden recommends
httpOnly cookies for token storage to protect against XSS. He is right in a production
context. We chose localStorage anyway for this academic prototype and acknowledged
it explicitly in the codebase as a known shortcut.

assumption that the structural patterns transfer seems reasonable – finite slots, forgetful

SOFTWARE REQUIREMENTS SPECIFICATION (SRS)
3.1 Purpose and Scope
The requirements documented here changed three times before we finalised them,
and understanding why they changed is as useful as knowing what they ended up
being. The SRS format follows conventions described in Pressman and Maxim [7] and
Sommerville [11]. The requirements came from three sources: the literature review,
two conversations with potential users (the clinic receptionist and salon owner from
Chapter 1), and a dry run with two classmates who tried using an early paper prototype.
Several requirements have backstories attached to them below – not for decoration, but
because a requirement without a reason for existing is usually a requirement that will
get misimplemented.

3.2 User Roles
• Customer: Any individual who registers to browse providers and book appointments. Registration is self-service and immediate.
• Service Provider (Admin): A business owner or manager who registers their
business, sets up services and staff, and manages bookings through the admin
dashboard.
• Staff Member: A person assigned by the provider to specific services and working
hours, bookable by customers when selecting who to see.

3.3 Functional Requirements
3.3.1 Customer Requirements
FR-C1. The system shall allow customers to register with email and password or via
Google OAuth.
FR-C2. Registered customers shall be able to search for service providers by name or
category.
FR-C3. The system shall display real-time slot availability for a selected service, staff
member, and date.
11

CHAPTER 3. SRS

Slotify – Smart Appointment Booking System

FR-C4. Customers shall be able to book an available slot and receive an email confirmation
within 60 seconds.
FR-C5. Customers shall be able to reschedule an existing booking to a different available
slot.
FR-C5a (sub-requirement): When rescheduling, the system shall display the three
nearest alternative slots automatically. This emerged from our paper prototype
dry run – one of our two test participants spent 45 seconds scrolling through an
empty calendar trying to find the next available time after their first choice was
taken. That single observation made the auto-suggest behaviour non-negotiable.
FR-C6. Customers shall be able to cancel a booking, with the slot immediately becoming
available to others.
FR-C7. The system shall send an automated reminder email 24 hours before a scheduled
appointment.
FR-C8. Customers shall be able to view their complete booking history from their profile
page.
Writing the customer requirements first created an implicit constraint on the provider
requirements that we hadn’t fully anticipated. Every feature we gave customers –
rescheduling, cancellation, history – required a corresponding data-access decision on
the provider side. FR-C6 (cancellation releasing the slot immediately) sounds simple
until you ask what “immediately” means when a provider has set a minimum notice
period. That tension surfaced in our third design session and is directly why FR-P4

4.2 Database Design
The slot-window model used in Slotify is consistent with the fixed-duration structures
Bailey and Welch [1] identified as most practical for scheduled services, and refined
further by Gupta and Denton [4]. We started the modelling phase in PostgreSQL and
ran into the problem Sadalage and Fowler [9] predict: a shared appointments table
across different business types required either a wide nullable schema or a separate
metadata join that grew unwieldy. Switching to MongoDB let each business document
carry exactly the fields it needs.
A compound index on appointments (businessId, dateTime, status) is created at
application startup. We found the need for this empirically – with a test dataset of around
5,000 appointments, unindexed slot queries were taking close to two seconds. The
index brought that under 80ms.

4.5 Security Design
Authentication is stateless. On login, the server signs a JWT containing the user’s ID
and role using a secret stored as an environment variable. The token goes back to the
client and is stored in localStorage.
We are aware that localStorage is vulnerable to XSS attacks and that httpOnly cookies
are the more secure alternative – Madden [5] makes this case clearly. We acknowledged
this to each other at the time as a known shortcut rather than a proper solution. It sits in
the codebase as a TODO comment in the auth middleware, partly so we don’t forget it
and partly because explaining the limitation to Dr. Soni in the review session was easier
when the code itself admitted it. A production deployment would need to migrate to
httpOnly session cookies before going live with real user data.
Role-based access is enforced in middleware. A customer token cannot reach admin
routes. A provider token cannot access other providers’ data. This runs before any route
handler, not as an afterthought inside the handler itself.

4.6 UI Design Principles
The slot picker was the most iterated piece of UI in the whole project. The first version
used a dropdown list of times – simple to build, but it gave customers no sense of how
busy a provider was on a given day. Om noticed the problem during internal testing: a
Tuesday with twelve free slots and a Thursday with two looked identical in a dropdown
until you scrolled through it. He switched the component to a grid of time buttons – green
for available, greyed-out for taken – which makes density immediately visible. A provider
with two slots on a Thursday looks different from one with twelve, and that difference
turns out to affect booking behaviour in ways worth preserving.
We also considered Socket.io for real-time slot updates – if two customers are on the
same booking page simultaneously, the second one would see a slot disappear the
moment the first books. We decided a 300ms debounced API fetch was sufficient for
our target of small businesses with at most 30 concurrent users, without the overhead of
WebSocket connection management.

6.1.2 Slot Availability Logic
When a customer requests available slots for a specific date and service, the backend:
23

Slotify – Smart Appointment Booking System

CHAPTER 6. IMPLEMENTATION

1. Retrieves the provider’s working hours for that day of the week, including split-shift
configuration.
2. Generates all possible start times based on service duration (e.g., 9am–5pm at
45-minute intervals produces 9:00, 9:45, 10:30, and so on).
3. Queries appointments for all confirmed bookings on that date for the selected staff
member.
4. Removes any generated slot that overlaps with an existing booking or a configured
break period.
5. Returns only the remaining free slots.
The overlap check in step 4 is where we had the most trouble. The first version used
exact start-time matching – if an existing booking started at 3:00 PM, it would block
3:00 PM for new bookings. But a 60-minute service booked at 3:00 PM should also
block 3:30 PM for a 30-minute service, because the first service is still running. The first
version didn’t catch that partial-overlap case. Aksh found it by writing a test specifically
for the scenario: 60-minute booking at 3:00 PM, attempt to book a 30-minute slot at
3:30 PM. The test failed, confirming the suspicion. The fix was switching from equality
comparison to interval overlap detection: a new slot at time t with duration d is blocked if
any existing booking b satisfies b.start < t + d and b.end > t. That one line of logic
change is the reason the system works correctly.
The entire check happens at query time, not on a schedule, which means displayed
slots are always current.
6.1.3 Authentication Middleware
JWT verification runs as Express middleware before any protected route handler. It
extracts the token from the Authorization: Bearer header, verifies the signature, and
attaches the decoded user to req.user. Missing, expired, or tampered tokens return a
401 – the route handler never runs.
A separate role-check middleware verifies req.user.role === 'provider' for admin
routes, running as a second step in the chain after authentication rather than folded into
it. This keeps the two concerns testable independently.

6.1.4 Email Notification Service
Two email types are sent. Confirmation emails trigger immediately when an appointment
is created. Reminder emails are sent by a node-cron job that runs every hour and
queries for appointments where dateTime is between now + 23h and now + 25h and
reminderSent === false. For each match, the email goes out and reminderSent is

set to true to prevent duplicates.
The two-hour window (23–25 hours rather than exactly 24) is intentional: an hourly
job running against a 24-hour exact window would miss appointments that land in the
gap depending on when they were created. In the four weeks of testing we ran, no
appointment missed its reminder window, though a gap exactly at the hourly boundary
would be difficult to detect through normal testing – it would require an appointment
created at precisely the wrong minute. We acknowledge this as a theoretical gap rather
than an observed one.

8.1 What Comes Next
The work done this semester is a foundation, not a finished product. The items below are
not given equal weight intentionally – some are more commercially critical than others,
and the order here reflects that rather than treating everything as equally interesting.

8.2 Payment Integration
This is the most commercially important item and deserves more space than the others. When a customer prepays even a small deposit at the time of booking, no-show
rates drop – the mechanism is that financial commitment raises the perceived cost of
cancellation. This is supported by behavioural economics literature on commitment
devices, and is consistent with the pattern documented in [6] – the mechanism is simple:
financial commitment makes last-minute cancellation feel like it has a cost. The salon
owner we spoke to estimated she loses around 800–1000 rupees per week to empty
slots from no-shows. A refundable deposit of 100 rupees would change that calculus for
most customers without feeling punitive.
The plan is to integrate Razorpay for the Indian market and Stripe for broader use. Each
provider would configure their own policy: full payment at booking, partial deposit, or
no prepayment. The refund flow is where most of the engineering complexity lives –
it requires handling partial refunds, cancellation windows (full refund if cancelled 24
hours in advance, no refund within 4 hours), and edge cases like provider cancellations
where the customer should always get a full refund regardless. The payment integration
is what all of this hangs on, which is why it comes first in priority even though the AI
recommendations feature sounds more technically interesting.

8.3 Mobile Application
Most customers booking a salon or clinic appointment are on their phones. The web
app is responsive, but a native experience for something as interaction-heavy as date
selection and slot picking is noticeably better than a mobile browser. The plan is a React

8.4 AI-Based Slot Recommendations
This is the most technically ambitious item and the most uncertain. The basic idea is
using historical booking patterns to surface optimal slots – if most customers who book
haircuts on weekdays choose 10am–12pm windows, show those first.
The hard problem is cold start. A new business on Slotify has zero historical bookings to
learn from. The recommendation engine needs a fallback: suggest slots that are most
popular across all similar businesses in the same category. We don’t know yet at what
booking volume the personalised model starts to meaningfully outperform that heuristic.
That question needs an answer before this feature is worth building, which is part of why
it’s deferred. We’re not building it until we have enough real booking data to test whether
personalisation adds anything.

8.5 Advanced Analytics
The current analytics tab shows bookings per day and three summary numbers. The
harder problem here is not the queries – the data is already being stored in a shape that
would support richer analysis. It’s deciding what a salon owner actually wants to see
versus what we as developers think is analytically interesting.
When we asked the salon owner this question directly, her answer was immediate: she
wanted to know which of her staff members was getting the most repeat bookings. Not
total bookings – repeat bookings specifically. That’s a very different query from the

8.7 Multi-Branch Business Support
